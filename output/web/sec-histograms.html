    Section 1.4 Histograms

    Objectives

    • Describe the distribution of a quantitative variable using a histogram.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Read counts and intervals from a histogram.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Convert a count into a relative frequency and interpret it in context.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Apply the endpoint convention for assigning values to bins.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Explain how bin width affects the appearance of a distribution.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Our goal in data analysis is to describe patterns in data and build a useful summary of a group. A graph that summarizes the distribution of a variable lets us see two things at once: the possible values the variable takes, and how many individuals fall at each value or interval of values.
    🔗
    A dotplot does this well for small data sets. A histogram is an alternative that works far better for large ones. A histogram divides the variable values into equal-sized intervals and shows how many individuals fall in each one.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.1 From dots to bars

    Consider hip girth—the measurement around the hips—for \(507\) adults who exercise regularly. The measurements range from \(79\) cm to \(128\) cm.
    🔗
    A dotplot of \(507\) values is readable but crowded: each individual gets its own dot, and stacks near the center become tall and hard to count. The histogram fixes this in two steps.
    🔗
    Step one: choose bins. Divide the range into equal-sized intervals called bins. Here we use a bin width of \(5\) cm, starting at \(75\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step two: count and draw. Count how many individuals fall in each bin, then draw a bar whose height is that count.
    🔗
    The height of each bar is the count (also called the frequency) of individuals in that bin. Reading the graph: \(48\) adults have hip measurements between \(85\) and \(90\) cm, and \(97\) adults fall between \(100\) and \(105\) cm.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.2.

    In Figure 1.4.1, what does the height of a bar tell you?
    🔗
    • How many individuals have values falling inside that bin.
    • Correct. The height is the count, or frequency, for that interval.
    • The value of the variable for individuals in that bin.
    • The variable value is read off the horizontal axis. The height counts individuals.
    • The width of the interval that bin covers.
    • Every bin here has the same width, \(5\) cm. The bars differ in height, not width.
    • The average hip girth of individuals in that bin.
    • A histogram shows counts, not averages. Nothing in the graph reports a mean.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.3.

      A histogram is generally a better choice than a dotplot when the data set is large.
      🔗

    • True.

    • True. A dotplot draws one mark per individual, so with hundreds or thousands of values the stacks become impossible to read. A histogram groups individuals into bins, so the number of bars stays manageable no matter how many individuals there are.

    • False.

    • True. A dotplot draws one mark per individual, so with hundreds or thousands of values the stacks become impossible to read. A histogram groups individuals into bins, so the number of bars stays manageable no matter how many individuals there are.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.2 The endpoint convention

    A question comes up immediately. If one adult measures exactly \(90\) cm, does that person belong to the bin from \(85\) to \(90\text{,}\) or the bin from \(90\) to \(95\text{?}\) Both bins name \(90\) as an endpoint, and counting the person twice—or not at all—would be wrong.
    🔗
    Statisticians settle this with a convention, applied consistently to every bin.
    🔗

    Which bin gets the endpoint?

    A bin includes its left-hand endpoint and excludes its right-hand endpoint.
    🔗
    So the bin labeled \(85\) to \(90\) contains every individual with a value of \(85\) or more, up to but not including \(90\text{.}\) An adult measuring exactly \(90\) cm belongs to the next bin.
    🔗
    Every histogram in this course follows this rule.
    🔗
    🔗
    The convention is arbitrary—the opposite choice would work equally well—but applying it consistently guarantees that every individual is counted exactly once.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.4.

    An adult in this study has a hip girth of exactly \(100.0\) cm. Which bin counts this person?
    🔗
    • The bin from \(100\) to \(105\)
    • Correct. A bin includes its left endpoint, so \(100.0\) belongs to the bin that starts at \(100\text{.}\)
    • The bin from \(95\) to \(100\)
    • That bin excludes its right endpoint, so it stops just short of \(100\text{.}\) Review Which bin gets the endpoint?.
    • Both bins, since \(100\) is a shared boundary
    • Counting one person twice would make the bar heights sum to more than \(507\text{.}\) The convention exists precisely to prevent this.
    • Neither bin—boundary values are excluded
    • Then this adult would vanish from the graph. Every individual lands in exactly one bin.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.3 From counts to relative frequencies

    A count answers “how many,” but often the more useful question is “what fraction.” Knowing that \(48\) adults fall in a bin means little until you know whether the sample had \(60\) people or \(6000\text{.}\) Dividing the count by the total gives a relative frequency, usually reported as a percentage.
    🔗

    Example 1.4.6. Percentage in a single bin.

    Approximately what percentage of the sample has hip measurements between \(85\) and \(90\) cm?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Of the \(507\) adults, \(48\) fall in this bin.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{48}{507} \approx 0.095 = 9.5\% \end{equation*}
    About \(9.5\%\) of the adults in this sample have hip girths of at least \(85\) cm but less than \(90\) cm.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.4.7. Percentage across several bins.

    A pants manufacturer plans to produce three sizes of sweatpants. Size Large will fit hip girths of \(100\) cm or more. What percentage of the sample will wear size Large?
    🔗
    Solution.
    No single bin answers this, so add the counts for every bin at \(100\) cm and above:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 97 + 42 + 15 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 158 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{158}{507} \approx 0.312 = 31.2\% \end{equation*}
    About \(31.2\%\) of the adults in this sample will wear size Large sweatpants.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Spotlight on percentages.

    Percent means “per hundred.” A percentage describes a number as a fraction out of \(100\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 1—Identify the ratio. Fill in the blanks: (a part) out of (the group). The part is the subset with the special characteristic you care about.
    🔗
    Step 2—Calculate. Divide the part by the group size, then multiply by \(100\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 3—Interpret in context. For every \(100\) individuals in the group, this many will have the characteristic. Name the group and name the characteristic—a percentage with no context is not an answer.
    🔗
    🔗
    Applying that pattern to Example 1.4.7: the ratio is \(158\) out of \(507\) adults; the calculation gives \(31.2\%\text{;}\) and the interpretation is “for every \(100\) adults in this sample, about \(31\) will wear size Large.”
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.8.

    Using Table 1.4.5, approximately what percentage of the sample has a hip girth of at least \(90\) cm but less than \(95\) cm?
    🔗
    • About \(22.7\%\)
    • Correct: \(115 \div 507 \approx 0.227\text{.}\)
    • About \(115\%\)
    • \(115\) is the count, not a percentage. A percentage can never exceed \(100\%\) when the part is a subset of the group.
    • About \(4.4\%\)
    • This divides in the wrong direction (\(507 \div 115 \approx 4.4\text{,}\) which is not a percentage at all). Divide the part by the group.
    • About \(32.3\%\)
    • That is the bin from \(95\) to \(100\) cm. Check the row labels.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.9.

    Approximately what percentage of the sample has a hip girth of less than \(95\) cm?
    🔗
    • About \(36.5\%\)
    • Correct. Add the first four bins: \(2 + 20 + 48 + 115 = 185\text{,}\) and \(185 \div 507 \approx 0.365\text{.}\)
    • About \(22.7\%\)
    • That is only the bin from \(90\) to \(95\text{.}\) “Less than \(95\)” includes every bin below it as well.
    • About \(68.8\%\)
    • That is the percentage below \(100\) cm. You included one bin too many.
    • About \(63.5\%\)
    • This is the complement of the right answer—the percentage at or above \(95\) cm.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.10.

    You compute that \(19.1\%\) of this sample has a hip girth of at least \(100\) cm but less than \(105\) cm. Write one complete sentence interpreting that number for someone who has not seen the data. Name the group, name the characteristic, and make the sentence stand on its own.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Something like: “About \(19\) out of every \(100\) adults in this sample of regular exercisers have a hip girth of at least \(100\) cm but less than \(105\) cm.”
    🔗
    A complete interpretation names the group (adults in this sample who exercise regularly), the characteristic (hip girth in that interval), and the size of the share. Writing only “\(19.1\%\) of the data” restates the arithmetic without saying what it describes. Note also that this statement is about this sample, not about all adults everywhere.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.4 Two axes, one picture

    Because every relative frequency comes from dividing by the same total, rescaling the vertical axis from counts to percentages does not change the shape of the histogram at all. Only the numbers on the axis change.
    🔗
    This matters most when comparing two groups of different sizes. Suppose you wanted to compare hip girth for these \(507\) exercisers against a sample of \(80\) non-exercisers. Counts would be useless—every bar in the smaller group would be shorter simply because the group is smaller. Relative frequencies put both groups on the same scale, so differences in the bars mean differences in the distributions.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.12.

    Why would you use relative frequency rather than count when comparing the hip girth distributions of two samples of different sizes?
    🔗
    • Counts are affected by sample size, so the larger group would have taller bars regardless of its shape.
    • Correct. Dividing by the group total removes the effect of sample size, leaving only the shape of the distribution to compare.
    • Relative frequency changes the shape of the histogram to make differences easier to see.
    • The shape does not change at all—compare Figure 1.4.1 with Figure 1.4.11. Only the axis labels differ.
    • Counts cannot be computed when the sample sizes differ.
    • Counts are always computable. The problem is that they are not comparable across groups of different sizes.
    • Relative frequency is more precise than a count.
    • A relative frequency is derived from the count, so it carries no extra precision—rounding usually loses a little.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.4.5 When is a histogram the right graph?

    A histogram describes the distribution of a quantitative variable—one whose values are numbers that can be ordered and measured. Hip girth, exam score, household income, and reaction time all qualify.
    🔗
    A categorical variable, whose values are labels rather than measurements, calls for a bar graph instead. Eye color, academic major, and brand of phone have no natural numeric ordering, so there is nothing to divide into bins.
    🔗
    The visual difference reflects this. Histogram bars touch, because the bins are adjacent intervals on a continuous number line. Bar graph bars are drawn with gaps, because the categories are separate and their order on the page carries no meaning.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.13.

    A researcher records the academic major of each student in a study. Which graph should she use to display this variable?
    🔗
    • A bar graph, because major is a categorical variable.
    • Correct. Majors are labels, not measurements, so there are no intervals to bin.
    • A histogram, because she can count students in each major.
    • Being countable is not the same as being quantitative. A histogram needs values that can be divided into numeric intervals, and “biology” cannot be placed on a number line.
    • A histogram, after assigning each major a number.
    • Assigning arbitrary numbers to labels does not make the variable quantitative. The bins and the shape would be artifacts of whatever numbering was chosen.
    • Either one, since both display counts.
    • Both display counts, but a histogram also claims its horizontal axis is a meaningful numeric scale. For majors, that claim would be false.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.4.14.

    Look at Figure 1.4.1 once more. In two or three sentences, describe what it tells you about hip girth among these \(507\) adults. Where are most of the measurements concentrated, and is the picture symmetric or lopsided?
    🔗
    Solution.
    A good description might read: “Hip measurements for these \(507\) adults run from about \(79\) to \(128\) cm, with most values concentrated between \(90\) and \(105\) cm. The tallest bar covers \(95\) to \(100\) cm. The distribution is roughly bell-shaped but not quite symmetric: it has a longer tail stretching to the right, with a handful of adults measuring well above \(115\) cm and no matching group at the low end.”
    🔗
    The three things worth naming in any description of a distribution are where the values fall overall, where they cluster, and whether the two sides balance.
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗
    A histogram displays the distribution of a quantitative variable by dividing its values into equal-sized bins and drawing a bar whose height is the count, or frequency, of individuals in each bin. Bins include their left endpoint and exclude their right endpoint, so every individual is counted exactly once.
    🔗
    Dividing a count by the group total gives a relative frequency, usually written as a percentage. Relative frequencies allow comparison between groups of different sizes, and rescaling the vertical axis to show them does not change the shape of the graph.
    🔗
    Bin width is a choice made by whoever draws the graph. Wider bins smooth the distribution and hide detail; narrower bins reveal detail but can make random variation look meaningful.
