    Section 2.1 Introduction to the Normal Distribution

    Objectives

    • Recognize the shape and defining features of a normal distribution.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Identify the two parameters \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) from the notation \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • Describe how changing \(\mu\) or \(\sigma\) changes the graph.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Recognize situations where a normal model is not appropriate.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The normal distribution is the most important continuous distribution in statistics. It is also, as one textbook puts it, the most widely abused. Its graph is the familiar bell curve, and you will meet it in psychology, business, economics, nursing, the physical sciences, and mathematics. Many IQ scores are approximately normal. Real-estate prices often are. Some of your instructors may use a normal distribution to help determine your grade.
    🔗
    This chapter studies the normal distribution, the standard normal distribution, and their applications.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.1 Two parameters determine everything

    The normal distribution has exactly two parameters—two numerical descriptive measures: the mean \(\mu\) and the standard deviation \(\sigma\text{.}\) If \(X\) is a quantity with a normal distribution, we write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} X \sim N(\mu, \sigma) \end{equation*}
    Read this aloud as “\(X\) is normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\text{.}\)” Those two numbers are the whole specification. Nothing else about the distribution is left to choose.
    🔗

    Definition 2.1.1. Normal distribution notation.

    The notation \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)\) means that \(X\) is normally distributed with mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\text{.}\) For example, \(X \sim N(170, 6.28)\) describes a normal distribution with mean \(170\) and standard deviation \(6.28\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The probability density function—the formula that produces the curve—is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \, e^{-\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}} \end{equation*}
    It is a complicated function. Do not memorize it. You will never need to evaluate it by hand. It appears here only so you can see that \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are the only quantities in it that change from one normal distribution to the next.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.2.

    A statistician writes \(W \sim N(12, 4)\text{.}\) What is the standard deviation of \(W\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(4\)
    • Correct. In the notation \(N(\mu, \sigma)\text{,}\) the mean comes first and the standard deviation second.
    • \(12\)
    • That is the mean. The second number in the notation is the standard deviation.
    • \(2\text{,}\) since \(\sqrt{4} = 2\)
    • No square root is needed. The notation lists the standard deviation directly, not the variance.
    • There is not enough information.
    • There is. The two parameters in the notation completely determine a normal distribution—see Definition 2.1.1.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.2 Shape, symmetry, and the effect of the parameters

    The curve is symmetric about a vertical line drawn through the mean \(\mu\text{.}\) Because of that symmetry, the mean and the median of a normal distribution are the same number, and both sit at the peak of the curve.
    🔗
    Since the total area under any probability density curve must equal \(1\text{,}\) the two parameters act in different ways.
    🔗

    What each parameter controls.

    A change in \(\mu\) shifts the graph left or right without changing its shape.
    🔗
    A change in \(\sigma\) changes the shape: the curve becomes fatter and shorter as \(\sigma\) grows, skinnier and taller as \(\sigma\) shrinks. The area stays fixed at \(1\) either way.
    🔗
    🔗
    Because \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) can be any numbers at all, there are infinitely many normal distributions. One of them is special enough to get its own name: the standard normal distribution, with \(\mu = 0\) and \(\sigma = 1\text{.}\) The next section shows how every other normal distribution can be converted into that one.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.3.

    Two normal distributions have the same mean, but the first has \(\sigma = 2\) and the second has \(\sigma = 8\text{.}\) How do their graphs compare?
    🔗
    • They peak at the same place, but the second curve is wider and shorter.
    • Correct. Equal means put the peaks in the same place; the larger standard deviation spreads the area out, which forces the curve lower.
    • The second curve is shifted to the right of the first.
    • Shifting is what a change in \(\mu\) does. These two distributions have the same mean.
    • The second curve is wider and also taller, since it covers more area.
    • Both curves enclose area exactly \(1\text{.}\) A wider curve must therefore be shorter, not taller.
    • The graphs are identical, since the mean determines the curve.
    • It takes both parameters. Review What each parameter controls.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.4.

      For any normal distribution, the mean and the median are equal.
      🔗

    • True.

    • True. The curve is symmetric about a vertical line through \(\mu\text{,}\) so half the area lies on each side of the mean—which is exactly what makes it the median as well.

    • False.

    • True. The curve is symmetric about a vertical line through \(\mu\text{,}\) so half the area lies on each side of the mean—which is exactly what makes it the median as well.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.1.3 Where the normal model does not apply

    The normal distribution is extremely useful, but it cannot be applied to everything in the real world. A normal model assumes the data is symmetric about its center, with values trailing off at the same rate in both directions, and with no hard boundary nearby.
    🔗
    Plenty of real quantities violate this. Household income is strongly right-skewed: there is a floor at zero and a long tail of very high earners. The time until a lightbulb burns out is skewed as well. The number of children in a family is a count, not a continuous measurement, and takes only whole-number values. Applying a normal model to data like this produces answers that look authoritative and are wrong.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.1.5.

    Think of a quantity you could measure on the students at this college that would not be well described by a normal distribution. Name it, and in one sentence say what feature of it breaks the normal model.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Many answers work. Some examples:
    🔗
    Money spent on textbooks this semester: bounded below by zero and right-skewed, with most students clustered low and a few spending a great deal.
    🔗
    Number of siblings: a count that takes only whole-number values and cannot be negative.
    🔗
    Hours slept the night before an exam: often bounded above by physical limits and skewed by a group of students who barely slept.
    🔗
    The common features to look for are skew, a hard boundary near the bulk of the data, and whole-number-only values.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The normal distribution is continuous, bell-shaped, and symmetric about its mean. It is specified completely by two parameters: the mean \(\mu\) and the standard deviation \(\sigma\text{,}\) written \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)\text{.}\) Changing \(\mu\) shifts the graph; changing \(\sigma\) changes its width. Because the curve is symmetric, the mean and median coincide. The special case \(\mu = 0\text{,}\) \(\sigma = 1\) is the standard normal distribution, which the next section develops.
