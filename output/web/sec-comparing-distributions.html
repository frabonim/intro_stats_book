    Section 1.7 Comparing Two Distributions

    Objectives

    • Compare two distributions using histograms drawn on identical scales.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use a benchmark value to compare groups quantitatively.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Write a comparative paragraph that emphasizes differences between groups.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Distinguish an association observed in data from a causal claim.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    So far we have described one distribution at a time. Most interesting statistical questions involve a comparison: does this group differ from that one? Histograms answer such questions well, provided they are drawn carefully.
    🔗
    Our descriptions still focus on the overall pattern—shape, center, and spread—and on deviations from it. But comparison adds a tool: because histograms make percentages easy to compute, we can compare specific intervals of values directly.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.7.1 Smoking and birth weight

    Does smoking during pregnancy affect birth weight? To investigate, doctors collected data on \(189\) new mothers who gave birth at a hospital in Massachusetts during the \(1980\)s. Of these, \(115\) did not smoke during pregnancy and \(74\) did.
    🔗
    As always, left endpoints are included in the bin and right endpoints are not, so a baby weighing exactly \(1000\) grams belongs to the interval \(1000\) to \(1500\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Why the scales must match.

    Both histograms use the same horizontal range, the same bin width, and the same vertical scale. This is not decoration—it is what makes the comparison valid.
    🔗
    If the two graphs used different bin widths, differences in the bars might be artifacts of the binning rather than differences in the babies. If they used different vertical scales, a taller bar in one graph would not mean more babies. Matching all three lets you compare the pictures directly.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.4.

    Why is it important that Figure 1.7.2 and Figure 1.7.3 use identical axes?
    🔗
    • So that differences you see between the two pictures reflect differences in the data rather than differences in how the graphs were drawn.
    • Correct. With matched scales and bin widths, a visual difference is a real difference.
    • So that the two groups have the same number of individuals.
    • The groups have different sizes—\(115\) and \(74\)—and matching the axes does not change that.
    • Because histograms of the same variable are required to use the same bins.
    • No such requirement exists in general. It is a deliberate choice made here because we intend to compare.
    • So the percentages in each bin will be equal.
    • The percentages are not equal and are not expected to be. Finding where they differ is the whole point.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.7.2 Shape, center, and spread for each group

    Nonsmokers. The distribution of birth weights appears skewed slightly to the left. Weights for this group fall between approximately \(1000\) and \(5000\) grams, an overall range of about \(4000\) grams. Nearly half of these babies weighed between \(3000\) and \(4000\) grams:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{29 + 27}{115} = \frac{56}{115} \approx 0.487 = 48.7\% \end{equation*}
    with fewer babies in the lower weight ranges.
    🔗
    Smokers. The distribution appears slightly skewed to the right. Weights fall between approximately \(500\) and \(4500\) grams, also an overall range of about \(4000\) grams. Nearly half of these babies weighed between \(2000\) and \(3000\) grams:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{22 + 16}{74} = \frac{38}{74} \approx 0.514 = 51.4\% \end{equation*}
    with fewer babies in the heavier weight ranges.
    🔗
    Two things are worth noticing. The overall ranges are identical at about \(4000\) grams, so spread alone does not distinguish the groups. What differs is where the bulk of each distribution sits: the nonsmokers’ typical range is a full \(1000\) grams heavier.
    🔗
    A caution carried over from the previous sections: because the choice of bin width affects the shape of a histogram, we cannot make precise statements about center and spread here. Our sense of the “typical” range is itself affected by the binning.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.5.

    Both groups have an overall range of about \(4000\) grams. What does that tell you?
    🔗
    • The two groups are similarly variable, so range alone does not distinguish them—the difference lies elsewhere.
    • Correct. Equal ranges with different centers is exactly the pattern here: the smokers’ distribution is shifted lower, not narrower.
    • The two distributions are essentially the same.
    • Range is only one feature. The peaks sit a full \(1000\) grams apart, which is a substantial difference.
    • Smoking has no effect on birth weight.
    • The ranges match, but the locations of the distributions do not. Look at where each peak falls.
    • The two groups must be the same size.
    • Range says nothing about group size. These groups contain \(115\) and \(74\) mothers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.7.3 Comparing with a benchmark

    Another strategy for comparing distributions is to pick a meaningful cutoff value—a benchmark—and compute what percentage of each group falls beyond it. Benchmarks that come from outside the data, such as medical definitions, are especially persuasive because nobody can accuse you of choosing the cutoff to favor a conclusion.
    🔗

    Example 1.7.6. Low birth weight.

    Doctors define low birth weight as a weight below \(2500\) grams. Compare the percentage of smokers and nonsmokers whose babies met this definition.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Nonsmokers: \(3 + 8 + 18 = 29\) babies weighed less than \(2500\) grams.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{29}{115} \approx 0.252 = 25.2\% \end{equation*}
    Smokers: \(1 + 1 + 6 + 22 = 30\) babies weighed less than \(2500\) grams.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{30}{74} \approx 0.405 = 40.5\% \end{equation*}
    Note that the raw counts are nearly identical, \(29\) versus \(30\text{,}\) while the percentages differ enormously. This is why relative frequency matters when group sizes differ.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.7.7. Macrosomia.

    A condition called macrosomia, informally “big baby syndrome,” is defined as a birth weight of \(4000\) grams or more. Compare the two groups.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Nonsmokers: \(6 + 2 = 8\) babies weighed \(4000\) grams or more.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{8}{115} \approx 0.070 = 7.0\% \end{equation*}
    Smokers: only \(1\) baby weighed \(4000\) grams or more.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1}{74} \approx 0.014 = 1.4\% \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.8.

    In Example 1.7.6, \(29\) nonsmokers and \(30\) smokers had low-birth-weight babies—nearly the same count. Why is it misleading to stop there?
    🔗
    • The groups are different sizes, so the same count represents a much larger share of the smokers.
    • Correct. Thirty out of \(74\) is a far bigger fraction than \(29\) out of \(115\)—\(40.5\%\) versus \(25.2\%\text{.}\)
    • The counts were measured with different bin widths.
    • Both groups use identical bins. The issue is group size, not binning.
    • Counts are always less accurate than percentages.
    • Counts are exact. The problem is that they are not comparable across groups of different sizes.
    • The benchmark of \(2500\) grams is arbitrary.
    • It is a standard medical definition, not an arbitrary choice. And the issue here would remain at any cutoff.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.9.

    Using Table 1.7.1, approximately what percentage of babies born to nonsmokers weighed \(3000\) grams or more?
    🔗
    • About \(55.7\%\)
    • Correct: \(29 + 27 + 6 + 2 = 64\text{,}\) and \(64 \div 115 \approx 0.557\text{.}\)
    • About \(48.7\%\)
    • That is the percentage between \(3000\) and \(4000\) grams. “\(3000\) or more” also includes the two heaviest bins.
    • About \(64\%\)
    • \(64\) is the count, not the percentage. Divide by the group total of \(115\text{.}\)
    • About \(33.9\%\)
    • This divides by \(189\text{,}\) the total number of mothers in the study. When describing nonsmokers, the group is the \(115\) nonsmokers.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.7.4 Writing the comparison

    Now assemble everything into prose. The key difference from a single-group description: emphasize the comparison. A paragraph that describes group A fully, then describes group B fully, leaves the reader to do the comparing. Do it for them.
    🔗
    In this observational study, we compared mothers who smoked during pregnancy to mothers who did not. The variable is the birth weight of their babies.
    🔗
    Both groups showed a lot of variability in birth weight, with identical overall range estimates of about \(4000\) grams, and there was substantial overlap between the distributions. Nonsmokers had babies weighing between approximately \(1000\) and \(5000\) grams; smokers had babies weighing between approximately \(500\) and \(4500\) grams.
    🔗
    However, we observe important differences in the typical ranges for the two groups. For nonsmokers, nearly half the babies weighed between \(3000\) and \(4000\) grams (\(56\) of \(115\text{,}\) or \(48.7\%\)), with fewer babies in the lower ranges. For smokers, nearly half weighed between \(2000\) and \(3000\) grams (\(38\) of \(74\text{,}\) or \(51.4\%\)), with fewer babies in the heavier ranges.
    🔗
    Using the medical definition of low birth weight, below \(2500\) grams, smokers in this study had a much higher incidence: \(25.2\%\) (\(29\) of \(115\)) of babies born to nonsmokers met the definition, compared to \(40.5\%\) (\(30\) of \(74\)) of babies born to smokers. In this study, smoking is associated with lower birth weights, though the variability in the data suggests that other variables also contribute to birth weight.
    🔗
    🔗
    Read that last sentence again. It says smoking is associated with lower birth weights—not that smoking causes them. This is an observational study: nobody assigned mothers to smoke or not smoke. Mothers who smoke may differ from mothers who do not in many other ways that also affect birth weight. The data establishes a clear association, and stops there.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.10.

    Based on this study, which conclusion is appropriate?
    🔗
    • In this group of \(189\) mothers, smoking during pregnancy was associated with lower birth weights.
    • Correct. It describes an association, names the group studied, and claims nothing beyond what the data supports.
    • Smoking during pregnancy causes low birth weight.
    • This is an observational study, not an experiment. Mothers chose whether to smoke, and may differ in other ways that affect birth weight.
    • If a mother smokes, her baby will have low birth weight.
    • Nearly \(60\%\) of the smokers’ babies were not low birth weight. The association is about rates across a group, not a prediction for any individual.
    • Smoking has no relationship to birth weight, since both groups had the same range.
    • Equal ranges do not mean equal distributions. The low-birth-weight rate differs by more than \(15\) percentage points.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.11.

    Using Example 1.7.7, write two sentences comparing the rate of macrosomia between the two groups. Include both percentages, and be careful about what the comparison does and does not establish.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Something like: “Babies born to nonsmoking mothers were about five times as likely to meet the definition of macrosomia: \(7.0\%\) (\(8\) of \(115\)) compared to \(1.4\%\) (\(1\) of \(74\)) for smokers. However, this comparison rests on a single macrosomic baby in the smoking group, so the difference should be treated cautiously.”
    🔗
    The second sentence is what separates a good answer from an adequate one. A percentage built on a count of one is fragile—if that single baby had weighed slightly less, the smokers’ rate would be \(0\%\text{.}\) Reporting the underlying counts alongside the percentages lets a reader see this for themselves.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.7.5 Dotplots and histograms compared

    We now have two graphs for the distribution of a quantitative variable. Neither is better in general; each highlights different aspects of the data.
    🔗
    There are no rules dictating which to use. With a small data set where individual values matter, a dotplot. With hundreds of observations, or when you intend to work with percentages, a histogram.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.13.

    A researcher has reaction times for \(12\) participants and wants readers to be able to see each individual value, including the exact fastest and slowest times. Which graph should she use?
    🔗
    • A dotplot, since individual values stay visible and the exact range can be read off.
    • Correct. With only \(12\) values there is no crowding problem, and binning would hide exactly what she wants to show.
    • A histogram, since it is the more standard graph.
    • A histogram would group her twelve values into a handful of bars, hiding the individual times and leaving the exact range unreadable.
    • A histogram with a very small bin width, so each value gets its own bar.
    • At that point you have built a dotplot the hard way, with a graph that invites readers to misjudge the empty bins.
    • Either one, since both display the same information.
    • They do not. A histogram discards individual values by design, which is precisely what she needs to avoid here.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.7.14.

      You can read the exact overall range of a data set from its histogram.
      🔗

    • True.

    • False. A histogram tells you only which bin the smallest and largest values fall into. In Figure 1.7.2 we can say the lightest baby weighed somewhere between \(1000\) and \(1500\) grams, but not exactly how much. A dotplot shows the actual values, so the range can be computed exactly.

    • False.

    • False. A histogram tells you only which bin the smallest and largest values fall into. In Figure 1.7.2 we can say the lightest baby weighed somewhere between \(1000\) and \(1500\) grams, but not exactly how much. A dotplot shows the actual values, so the range can be computed exactly.
    🔗
    🔗
    To compare the distributions of a quantitative variable for two groups, draw histograms on identical horizontal scales, with identical bin widths and identical vertical scales. Describe the shape, center, and spread of each, note any outliers, and—most importantly—say explicitly how the groups differ.
    🔗
    Use percentages rather than counts whenever the groups are different sizes. A benchmark value drawn from outside the data, such as a medical threshold, makes a comparison concrete and hard to dispute.
    🔗
    Finally, describe what the data shows and no more. An observational study can establish that two things are associated. It cannot, on its own, establish that one causes the other.
