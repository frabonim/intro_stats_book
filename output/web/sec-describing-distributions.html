    Section 1.6 Describing a Distribution

    Objectives

    • Describe the shape of a distribution as skewed, symmetric, or uniform.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Identify the approximate center and spread of a distribution from a histogram.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Recognize outliers as values falling outside the overall pattern.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Write a paragraph summarizing a distribution, supported by percentages.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Reading a histogram is a skill; writing down what you read is a separate one. This section builds a routine for turning a graph into a paragraph. The routine has four parts, always in the same order: shape, center, spread, and outliers.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.6.1 A worked example: ages of Oscar winners

    Here are the ages of the actresses who won the Academy Award for Best Actress each year from \(1970\) through \(2001\)—thirty-two winners in all.
    🔗
    \(34\text{,}\) \(34\text{,}\) \(26\text{,}\) \(37\text{,}\) \(42\text{,}\) \(41\text{,}\) \(35\text{,}\) \(31\text{,}\) \(41\text{,}\) \(33\text{,}\) \(30\text{,}\) \(74\text{,}\) \(33\text{,}\) \(49\text{,}\) \(38\text{,}\) \(61\text{,}\) \(21\text{,}\) \(41\text{,}\) \(26\text{,}\) \(80\text{,}\) \(42\text{,}\) \(29\text{,}\) \(33\text{,}\) \(35\text{,}\) \(45\text{,}\) \(49\text{,}\) \(39\text{,}\) \(34\text{,}\) \(26\text{,}\) \(25\text{,}\) \(33\text{,}\) \(35\)
    🔗
    Now work through the four parts in order.
    🔗
    Shape. The distribution of ages appears skewed to the right. Most winners are young: \(91\%\) (\(29\) of \(32\)) were under \(50\text{,}\) and \(56\%\) (\(18\) of \(32\)) were \(35\) or younger. The long thin tail stretching toward \(80\) is what makes the distribution right-skewed.
    🔗
    Center. The distribution appears centered between \(30\) and \(35\) years. That single bin holds \(28\%\) (\(9\) of \(32\)) of the winners, more than any other.
    🔗
    Spread. The ages run from about \(20\) to about \(80\text{,}\) so the overall range is approximately \(60\) years. There is a great deal of variability in the ages of Best Actress winners.
    🔗
    Outliers. Winners older than \(60\) are unusual. There are three such values, one each in the intervals \(60\)–\(65\text{,}\) \(70\)–\(75\text{,}\) and \(80\)–\(85\text{.}\) Each is separated from the main body of the data by at least one empty bin.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.6.2.

    Why is the distribution in Figure 1.6.1 described as skewed to the right rather than to the left?
    🔗
    • The long, thin tail of the distribution extends toward the higher values on the right.
    • Correct. Skew is named for the direction of the tail, not the direction of the bulk of the data.
    • Most of the data is piled up on the left side of the graph.
    • That is a true observation, and it does accompany right skew, but it is not the definition. Skew is named for the tail.
    • The tallest bar is to the right of the center of the graph.
    • The tallest bar is at \(30\) to \(35\text{,}\) well to the left of the midpoint of the axis.
    • The values increase from left to right along the axis.
    • That is true of every histogram and has nothing to do with skew.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.6.3.

    In this data set, the winner aged \(80\) is identified as an outlier. What does that mean?
    🔗
    • Her age falls well outside the overall pattern of the rest of the data.
    • Correct. An outlier is a value that stands apart from the pattern, separated from the main body of the distribution.
    • Her age was recorded incorrectly and should be removed.
    • An outlier may be a recording error, but it may also be a perfectly real and interesting value. Jessica Tandy really did win at \(80\text{.}\) Never delete a value just because it is unusual.
    • She is the oldest person in the data set.
    • She is, but being the maximum does not by itself make a value an outlier. The \(49\)-year-old winners are near the top of the main group and are not outliers.
    • Her age is more than twice the mean age.
    • No such rule defines an outlier. The judgment is about whether the value sits outside the overall pattern.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.6.2 Turning observations into a paragraph

    Four separate observations are not yet a description. The next step is to assemble them into prose that someone who has not seen the graph could follow.
    🔗
    Here is a plain-language version:
    🔗
    Between \(1970\) and \(2001\text{,}\) the Oscar for Best Actress was awarded most often to young actresses: \(56\%\) (\(18\) of \(32\)) of the winners were \(35\) or younger, with \(28\%\) (\(9\) of \(32\)) between \(30\) and \(35\) years of age. Winners ranged in age from about \(20\) to about \(80\text{,}\) but only \(3\) of the \(32\) were over \(60\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    And here is the same description using the formal vocabulary of this chapter:
    🔗
    Between \(1970\) and \(2001\text{,}\) the Oscar for Best Actress was awarded most often to young actresses. The distribution of ages is skewed to the right: \(56\%\) (\(18\) of \(32\)) of the winners were \(35\) or younger, with the center of the distribution between \(30\) and \(35\) years of age. With winners ranging in age from about \(20\) to about \(80\text{,}\) the overall range of the distribution is about \(60\text{.}\) But much of this variability is due to three outliers who were older than \(60\) when they won.
    🔗
    🔗
    Both paragraphs are good. Notice what they have in common: every claim about the shape is backed by a number, and every number is attached to a unit and a group. “Skewed to the right” on its own is an assertion. “Skewed to the right: \(56\%\) of winners were \(35\) or younger” is evidence.
    🔗

    Describing a distribution.

    Shape. Skewed left, skewed right, symmetric with a central peak, or roughly uniform. Not every distribution fits one of these neatly—say so when it does not.
    🔗
    Center. A typical value, or the interval where the data clusters.
    🔗
    Spread. How much the values vary. The overall range (largest minus smallest) is one measure; a typical range covering most of the data is another.
    🔗
    Outliers. Any values falling outside the overall pattern.
    🔗
    Then write it as prose, supporting each claim with a percentage.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.6.4.

    Which of these is the strongest description of the Oscar age distribution?
    🔗
    • “The distribution is skewed right; \(29\) of the \(32\) winners (\(91\%\)) were under \(50\text{,}\) with three older outliers at \(61\text{,}\) \(74\text{,}\) and \(80\text{.}\)”
    • Correct. It names the shape, supports it with a percentage, and identifies the outliers specifically.
    • “The distribution is skewed right.”
    • True but unsupported. A reader has no way to judge how pronounced the skew is.
    • “The bars are \(1\text{,}\) \(5\text{,}\) \(9\text{,}\) \(6\text{,}\) \(5\text{,}\) \(3\text{,}\) \(0\text{,}\) \(0\text{,}\) \(1\text{,}\) \(0\text{,}\) \(1\text{,}\) \(0\text{,}\) \(1\text{.}\)”
    • This reports the graph without describing it. A description tells the reader what the pattern means.
    • “Young actresses win more Oscars because Hollywood discriminates against older women.”
    • This leaps from description to causal explanation. The data shows the pattern; it does not establish why the pattern exists.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.6.5.

    Suppose a histogram of the amount students spent on textbooks this semester is roughly symmetric, centered near \(\$300\text{,}\) with values ranging from \(\$50\) to \(\$550\) and no gaps or isolated bars.
    🔗
    Write a two- or three-sentence description of this distribution using the shape-center-spread-outliers routine.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Something like: “The distribution of textbook spending is roughly symmetric with a single central peak. Spending is centered near \(\$300\text{,}\) and values range from about \(\$50\) to about \(\$550\text{,}\) an overall range of roughly \(\$500\text{.}\) There are no outliers—no student’s spending falls outside the overall pattern.”
    🔗
    Saying explicitly that there are no outliers is worth doing. A reader cannot tell the difference between “I checked and found none” and “I forgot to look” unless you say so.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    To describe the distribution of a quantitative variable, report the overall pattern—shape, center, and spread—and any deviations from that pattern, meaning outliers.
    🔗
    Shape may be skewed left, skewed right, symmetric with a central peak, or uniform, and some distributions fit none of these. Center is a typical value representing the group. Spread describes how the data varies; the overall range is one measure. Outliers are values falling outside the overall pattern.
    🔗
    Assemble these observations into a paragraph, and support each claim about shape with a percentage. A description without numbers is an opinion.
