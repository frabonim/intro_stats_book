    Section 1.5 How Bins Shape the Picture

    Objectives

    • Explain how bin width and starting point change the appearance of a histogram.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Recognize that shape, center, and spread read from a histogram are partly artifacts of binning.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Identify histograms whose bins are too wide or too narrow to be useful.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Choose a bin width appropriate to the context of the data.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    We now have two graphs that summarize the distribution of a quantitative variable: the dotplot and the histogram. From a dotplot we described the pattern in the data with statements about shape, center, and spread.
    🔗
    We have to be more careful making the same statements from a histogram. A dotplot shows every individual value, so its appearance is fixed by the data. A histogram shows grouped values, and the grouping is a choice. Our perception of shape, center, and spread can change depending on how the bins are defined.
    🔗

    Subsection 1.5.1 The same data, three ways

    Twenty-one students took an exam. Here are their scores:
    🔗
    \(46\text{,}\) \(48\text{,}\) \(52\text{,}\) \(55\text{,}\) \(57\text{,}\) \(58\text{,}\) \(63\text{,}\) \(66\text{,}\) \(68\text{,}\) \(69\text{,}\) \(70\text{,}\) \(71\text{,}\) \(72\text{,}\) \(77\text{,}\) \(78\text{,}\) \(82\text{,}\) \(84\text{,}\) \(85\text{,}\) \(88\text{,}\) \(93\text{,}\) \(96\)
    🔗
    Below are three histograms built from exactly these twenty-one numbers. Nothing about the data changed between them.
    🔗
    These are not small differences. Figure 1.5.1 is perfectly symmetric. Figure 1.5.3 leans right. Reading Figure 1.5.2, you would say most students scored in the \(70\)s. Reading Figure 1.5.3, you would say typical students scored between \(65\) and \(75\text{.}\) Same twenty-one students.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.4.

      All three histograms above display exactly the same twenty-one exam scores.
      🔗

    • True.

    • True, and this is the point of the section. Nothing about the data changed. Only the bin width and the starting point of the first bin changed, and that was enough to alter the apparent shape of the distribution.

    • False.

    • True, and this is the point of the section. Nothing about the data changed. Only the bin width and the starting point of the first bin changed, and that was enough to alter the apparent shape of the distribution.

    Subsection 1.5.2 Why the grouping changes the picture

    Five students scored in the \(70\)s: \(70\text{,}\) \(71\text{,}\) \(72\text{,}\) \(77\text{,}\) and \(78\text{.}\) Follow just those five through the three graphs.
    🔗
    In Figure 1.5.1, all five land in the middle bin together with the four students who scored \(63\text{,}\) \(66\text{,}\) \(68\text{,}\) and \(69\text{.}\) They disappear into a group of nine.
    🔗
    In Figure 1.5.2, those five form a bin entirely of their own, because no other student scored between \(70\) and \(80\text{.}\) That is the highlighted bar, and it is what makes the \(70\)s look like the typical performance.
    🔗
    In Figure 1.5.3, the five get split: \(70\text{,}\) \(71\text{,}\) and \(72\) fall in the bin from \(65\) to \(75\text{,}\) while \(77\) and \(78\) fall in the bin from \(75\) to \(85\text{.}\) The group of five never appears as a group at all.
    🔗
    When we change the bins, the data gets grouped differently, and the different grouping changes the appearance of the histogram. Nothing deceptive is happening. Every one of these graphs is an accurate count.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.5.

    Why do the five students who scored in the \(70\)s form their own bar in Figure 1.5.2 but not in Figure 1.5.3?
    🔗
    • The shifted bins cut through the middle of the group, splitting it across two bars.
    • Correct. The boundary at \(75\) falls between \(72\) and \(77\text{,}\) dividing a group that the other binning kept together.
    • The shifted histogram uses a wider bin, so more students are absorbed into each bar.
    • Both histograms use a bin width of \(10\text{.}\) Only the starting point changed.
    • Some of those students are excluded from the shifted histogram.
    • Every student appears in every histogram. All three have bars summing to \(21\text{.}\)
    • The shifted histogram sorts the scores differently before binning.
    • Binning does not depend on the order of the data. Each value is simply placed into whichever interval contains it.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 1.5.3 Choosing a bin width

    If three defensible choices give three different pictures, which one should we use? There is no formula, but there are two failure modes to avoid.
    🔗

    Two ways to go wrong.

    Bins too wide. Grouping the data into only a few bins produces a “skyscraper”—a couple of towering bars that say almost nothing about variability within the group.
    🔗
    Bins too narrow. Grouping the data into very many bins produces a “pancake”—a long row of bars one or two units tall, where random gaps look like real features and no pattern is visible.
    🔗
    Between those extremes, prefer a bin width that means something in context.
    🔗
    🔗
    That last point is what settles the exam-score example. Of the three graphs, Figure 1.5.2 is the most useful summary—not because \(10\)-point bins are mathematically optimal, but because \(10\)-point intervals starting at round numbers correspond to letter grades. The bins carry meaning the reader already understands.
    🔗
    A good practice is to look at more than one binning before describing a distribution, and to be suspicious of any feature that appears under one choice and vanishes under another.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.6.

    A student builds a histogram of \(400\) commute times using just two bins. What is the problem?
    🔗
    • Two bins hide almost all the variability—the graph is a “skyscraper” that says little more than a single count would.
    • Correct. With bins that wide, individuals with very different commute times get lumped together, and the shape of the distribution is invisible.
    • Histograms are required to have at least five bins.
    • There is no such rule. The guidance in Two ways to go wrong is about usefulness, not a minimum count.
    • The bar heights will not add up to \(400\text{.}\)
    • They will. Every individual lands in exactly one bin no matter how wide the bins are.
    • Two bins will make the distribution look skewed when it is symmetric.
    • The real problem is more basic: with two bars there is essentially no shape to read at all, skewed or otherwise.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.7.

    A researcher graphs the ages of \(60\) survey respondents using a bin width of one year, producing about \(50\) bars that are mostly \(0\text{,}\) \(1\text{,}\) or \(2\) units tall. What should she do?
    🔗
    • Widen the bins, so the overall pattern becomes visible.
    • Correct. This is the “pancake” problem. With \(60\) values spread over \(50\) bins, the bar heights are mostly noise.
    • Narrow the bins further, to show even more detail.
    • That makes it worse. The graph already shows more detail than \(60\) observations can support.
    • Remove the bins with a count of zero.
    • Deleting empty bins would break the numeric scale on the horizontal axis and misrepresent where the gaps in the data are.
    • Collect more data, since histograms require large samples.
    • More data would help, but it is not necessary. A sensible bin width will give a readable histogram of these \(60\) values.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 1.5.8.

    Suppose you are graphing the annual household incomes of families in a county, where values range from near \(\$0\) to about \(\$400{,}000\text{.}\) Propose a bin width, and give one sentence defending it. What makes your choice meaningful to a reader?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Many answers are defensible. A bin width of \(\$25{,}000\) gives about sixteen bars across the range—enough to show shape without becoming a pancake—and the boundaries fall at round figures that readers can hold in their heads.
    🔗
    A bin width of \(\$10{,}000\) would give forty bars, which is probably too many unless the county is large. A bin width of \(\$100{,}000\) would give four, which is a skyscraper.
    🔗
    The strongest answers do what the exam-score example did: tie the bin boundaries to something the audience already recognizes, such as federal poverty thresholds or tax brackets.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Descriptions of shape, center, and spread read from a histogram are affected by how the bins are defined. Changing the bin width, or keeping the width and shifting the starting point, regroups the data and can change the apparent shape of the distribution.
    🔗
    Avoid bins so wide that the graph becomes a “skyscraper” hiding all variability, and bins so narrow that it becomes a “pancake” showing only noise. When a bin width has meaning in context, prefer it.
    🔗
    A dotplot does not have this problem, because it shows every individual value. That is the trade: the histogram handles large data sets, and pays for it with a dependence on the binning choice.
