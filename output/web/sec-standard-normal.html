    Section 2.2 The Standard Normal Distribution

    Objectives

    • Compute a \(z\)-score from a value, a mean, and a standard deviation.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Interpret a \(z\)-score as a number of standard deviations above or below the mean.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Recover an original value from a \(z\)-score.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Apply the Empirical Rule to a normal distribution.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Two exam scores, \(72\) and \(58\text{,}\) tell you almost nothing on their own. If the first exam had a mean of \(85\) and the second a mean of \(45\text{,}\) the lower raw score is the better performance. Comparing values across different distributions requires putting them on a common scale, and this section builds that scale.
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.1 Standardizing: the \(z\)-score

    The standard normal distribution is a normal distribution of standardized values called \(z\)-scores. A \(z\)-score is measured in units of the standard deviation. If a distribution has mean \(5\) and standard deviation \(2\text{,}\) then the value \(11\) sits three standard deviations above the mean, since \(11 = 5 + 3(2)\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 2.2.1. \(z\)-score.

    If \(X\) is normally distributed with \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)\text{,}\) the \(z\)-score of a value \(x\) is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \end{equation*}
    The \(z\)-score reports how many standard deviations \(x\) lies above or below \(\mu\text{.}\) Values above the mean have positive \(z\)-scores; values below have negative \(z\)-scores. If \(x = \mu\text{,}\) then \(z = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    The transformation in Definition 2.2.1 produces a new distribution \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\)—mean \(0\text{,}\) standard deviation \(1\)—no matter what \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) you started with.
    🔗

    Example 2.2.2. Computing two \(z\)-scores.

    Suppose \(X \sim N(5, 6)\text{,}\) so \(\mu = 5\) and \(\sigma = 6\text{.}\) Find and interpret the \(z\)-scores for \(x = 17\) and \(x = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    For \(x = 17\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z = \frac{17 - 5}{6} = 2 \end{equation*}
    So \(17\) is two standard deviations to the right of the mean. Check by reversing: \(5 + (2)(6) = 17\text{.}\)
    🔗
    For \(x = 1\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z = \frac{1 - 5}{6} \approx -0.67 \end{equation*}
    So \(1\) is about \(0.67\) standard deviations to the left of the mean. Check: \(5 + (-0.67)(6) \approx 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.3.

    A student computes a \(z\)-score of \(z = -1.8\) for her value. What does this tell her?
    🔗
    • Her value is \(1.8\) standard deviations below the mean.
    • Correct. The negative sign places the value below the mean, and the size \(1.8\) counts standard deviations.
    • Her value is \(1.8\) standard deviations above the mean.
    • Check the sign. A negative \(z\)-score means the value is below the mean.
    • Her value is \(1.8\) units below the mean.
    • Close, but a \(z\)-score counts standard deviations, not the original units. If \(\sigma = 10\text{,}\) then \(z = -1.8\) means \(18\) units below the mean.
    • Her value is \(1.8\) times smaller than the mean.
    • A \(z\)-score is not a ratio. It measures distance from the mean in units of \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 2.2.4. Weight change.

    Some doctors believe a person can lose five pounds on average in a month by reducing fat intake and exercising. Let \(X\) be the weight lost in pounds in one month, with \(X \sim N(5, 2)\text{.}\) Interpret the \(z\)-scores for a person who lost \(10\) pounds and a person who gained three pounds.
    🔗
    Solution.
    For a loss of \(10\) pounds, \(x = 10\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z = \frac{10 - 5}{2} = 2.5 \end{equation*}
    This person is \(2.5\) standard deviations to the right of the mean.
    🔗
    A gain of three pounds is a loss of \(-3\) pounds, so \(x = -3\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z = \frac{-3 - 5}{2} = -4 \end{equation*}
    This person is four standard deviations to the left of the mean—a genuinely unusual outcome.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.5.

      In Example 2.2.4, a negative value of \(x\) is meaningful: it represents weight gained rather than lost.
      🔗

    • True.

    • Right. The variable records weight lost, so a negative amount lost is a gain. Always ask what the variable measures before deciding whether a value is possible.

    • False.

    • Right. The variable records weight lost, so a negative amount lost is a gain. Always ask what the variable measures before deciding whether a value is possible.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.2 Comparing values across distributions

    Suppose \(X \sim N(5, 6)\) and \(Y \sim N(2, 1)\text{.}\) We found that \(x = 17\) gives \(z = 2\text{.}\) For \(y = 4\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} z = \frac{4 - 2}{1} = 2 \end{equation*}
    Both values sit exactly two standard deviations to the right of their own means, even though \(17\) and \(4\) are nowhere near each other numerically. This is what \(z\)-scores are for. If \(X\) records weight gain for one group and \(Y\) records weight gain for a second group, then \(x = 17\) and \(y = 4\) represent the same standardized gain relative to their own groups.
    🔗

    Example 2.2.6. Heights in Chile, 2009–2010.

    The mean height of 15- to 18-year-old males from Chile in 2009–2010 was \(170\) cm with standard deviation \(6.28\) cm, so \(X \sim N(170, 6.28)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Interpret the \(z\)-score for a male who is \(168\) cm tall.
    🔗
    (b) A male has \(z = 1.27\text{.}\) How tall is he?
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) \(z = \dfrac{168 - 170}{6.28} \approx -0.32\text{,}\) so \(168\) cm is about \(0.32\) standard deviations below the mean.
    🔗
    (b) Solving \(z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\) for \(x\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x = \mu + z\sigma \end{equation*}
    so \(x = 170 + (1.27)(6.28) = 177.98\) cm.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Two forms of one relationship.

    From a value to a \(z\)-score: \(z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    From a \(z\)-score back to a value: \(x = \mu + z\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗
    These are the same equation. Which one you use depends on which quantity you are missing.
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.7.

    A distribution has \(\mu = 64\) and \(\sigma = 2.5\text{.}\) You are told a value has \(z\)-score \(z = -0.8\) and asked for the value itself. Which computation gives the answer?
    🔗
    • \(64 + (-0.8)(2.5)\)
    • Correct—this is \(x = \mu + z\sigma\text{,}\) giving \(x = 62\text{.}\)
    • \(\dfrac{-0.8 - 64}{2.5}\)
    • That is the standardizing formula, but you already have the \(z\)-score. You need to go the other direction.
    • \(64 - \dfrac{0.8}{2.5}\)
    • The \(z\)-score is multiplied by \(\sigma\text{,}\) not divided by it.
    • \((-0.8)(64) + 2.5\)
    • Review the box Two forms of one relationship. The mean is added, and the \(z\)-score multiplies the standard deviation.
    🔗

    Example 2.2.8. Comparing two generations.

    From 1984 to 1985, the mean height of 15- to 18-year-old Chilean males was \(172.36\) cm with standard deviation \(6.34\) cm, so \(Y \sim N(172.36, 6.34)\text{.}\) Recall \(X \sim N(170, 6.28)\) for 2009–2010.
    🔗
    Compare a 2009 male of height \(160.58\) cm with a 1984 male of height \(162.85\) cm.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} z_x = \frac{160.58 - 170}{6.28} = -1.5 \qquad z_y = \frac{162.85 - 172.36}{6.34} = -1.5 \end{equation*}
    Both fall exactly \(1.5\) standard deviations below their own means. Relative to their own generations, these two heights are equally short—even though the raw measurements differ by more than two centimeters.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.9.

    In Example 2.2.8, the 1984 male is taller in centimeters than the 2009 male, yet both have the same \(z\)-score. In one or two sentences, explain to a classmate how both of those statements can be true at the same time.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The two men are being measured against different reference groups. The 1984 population was taller on average (\(\mu = 172.36\) versus \(\mu = 170\)), so a taller raw height is needed to occupy the same relative position. A \(z\)-score describes position within a distribution, not absolute size, so identical \(z\)-scores mean identical standing in their respective populations.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.2.3 The Empirical Rule

    Because every normal distribution becomes the same standard normal distribution after standardizing, one set of percentages applies to all of them.
    🔗

    Theorem 2.2.10. The Empirical Rule.

    If \(X \sim N(\mu, \sigma)\text{,}\) then approximately:
    🔗
    • \(68\%\) of values lie between \(\mu - \sigma\) and \(\mu + \sigma\text{,}\) that is, between \(z = -1\) and \(z = 1\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(95\%\) of values lie between \(\mu - 2\sigma\) and \(\mu + 2\sigma\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    • \(99.7\%\) of values lie between \(\mu - 3\sigma\) and \(\mu + 3\sigma\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    Almost every value lies within three standard deviations of the mean.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.2.11. Applying the rule.

    Suppose \(X\) is normal with mean \(50\) and standard deviation \(6\text{.}\) Find the three Empirical Rule intervals.
    🔗
    Solution.
    About \(68\%\) lie between \(50 - 6 = 44\) and \(50 + 6 = 56\text{.}\)
    🔗
    About \(95\%\) lie between \(50 - 12 = 38\) and \(50 + 12 = 62\text{.}\)
    🔗
    About \(99.7\%\) lie between \(50 - 18 = 32\) and \(50 + 18 = 68\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Every one of these is just \(\mu \pm k\sigma\text{.}\) The Empirical Rule needs no table and no technology—only arithmetic.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.2.12.

    Scores on an entrance exam are approximately normal with \(\mu = 52\) points and \(\sigma = 11\) points. About \(95\%\) of scores lie between which two values?
    🔗
    • \(30\) and \(74\)
    • Correct. \(95\%\) corresponds to two standard deviations: \(52 \pm 2(11)\text{.}\)
    • \(41\) and \(63\)
    • That is \(\mu \pm 1\sigma\text{,}\) which captures about \(68\%\) of scores, not \(95\%\text{.}\)
    • \(19\) and \(85\)
    • That is \(\mu \pm 3\sigma\text{,}\) which captures about \(99.7\%\) of scores.
    • \(50\) and \(54\)
    • This interval is far too narrow. Each step out from the mean is a full standard deviation of \(11\) points.
    🔗

    🔗
    🔗
    A \(z\)-score is a standardized value. Standardizing any normal distribution produces \(Z \sim N(0, 1)\text{,}\) with mean \(0\) and standard deviation \(1\text{.}\) A \(z\)-score reports how many standard deviations a value lies above or below its mean, which makes values from different distributions directly comparable.
    🔗
    Standardize a value: \(z = \dfrac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Recover a value: \(x = \mu + z\sigma\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Empirical Rule: about \(68\%\text{,}\) \(95\%\text{,}\) and \(99.7\%\) of values lie within one, two, and three standard deviations of the mean.
