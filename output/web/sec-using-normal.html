    Section 2.3 Using the Normal Distribution

    Objectives

    • Interpret a normal probability as an area under the curve.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Use the complement rule to convert between left and right areas.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Choose between a probability calculation and a percentile calculation based on what the question gives you.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Find percentiles, quartiles, and middle intervals of a normal distribution.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    The previous section converted values into \(z\)-scores. This section puts that machinery to work answering the questions people actually ask: how likely is a value this large, and what value separates the top ten percent from everyone else?
    🔗

    Subsection 2.3.1 Probability is area

    For a continuous distribution, the probability that \(X\) falls below some value \(x\) is the area under the curve to the left of \(x\text{.}\) We write this \(P(X \lt x)\text{.}\) Normal tables, computers, and calculators all do the same job: they report that area.
    🔗
    Since the total area under the curve is \(1\text{,}\) the area to the right follows immediately.
    🔗

    Left areas, right areas.

    \(P(X \lt x)\) is the area to the left of a vertical line through \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \(P(X \gt x) = 1 - P(X \lt x)\) is the area to the right.
    🔗
    For a continuous distribution, \(P(X \lt x)\) and \(P(X \le x)\) are the same number, and likewise for \(P(X \gt x)\) and \(P(X \ge x)\text{.}\) A single point has zero area, so including or excluding the endpoint changes nothing.
    🔗
    🔗
    That last point surprises people. In a continuous distribution, \(P(X = x) = 0\) for every single value of \(x\)—not because the value is impossible, but because a single point has no width and therefore no area.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3.1.

    For a normal distribution, you compute \(P(X \lt 65) = 0.6554\text{.}\) What is \(P(X \gt 65)\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • \(0.3446\)
    • Correct: \(1 - 0.6554 = 0.3446\text{.}\) The two areas must add to \(1\text{.}\)
    • \(0.6554\)
    • That is the area to the left. Unless \(65\) is the mean, the two sides are not equal.
    • \(-0.6554\)
    • A probability can never be negative. Subtract from \(1\) rather than negating.
    • It cannot be found without knowing \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\text{.}\)
    • The complement rule needs nothing beyond the left area. Review Left areas, right areas.
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3.2.

      For a normal distribution, \(P(X \lt 70)\) and \(P(X \le 70)\) are the same number.
      🔗

    • True.

    • True. The normal distribution is continuous, so the single point \(x = 70\) contributes zero area. Including or excluding the endpoint has no effect. This is not true for discrete distributions.

    • False.

    • True. The normal distribution is continuous, so the single point \(x = 70\) contributes zero area. Including or excluding the endpoint has no effect. This is not true for discrete distributions.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.3.2 Two kinds of question, two tools

    Nearly every normal distribution problem is one of two types, and telling them apart is most of the work.
    🔗
    On a TI-83+ or TI-84, both commands live under 2nd DISTR. The syntax is
    🔗
    normalcdf(lower value, upper value, mean, standard deviation)
    🔗
    invNorm(area to the left, mean, standard deviation)
    🔗
    For a one-sided area with normalcdf, you still need two endpoints, so use a number far out in the tail: 1E99 for the right end, -1E99 for the left. Enter 1E99 by pressing 1, then the EE key, then 99.
    🔗

    Example 2.3.4. Final exam scores.

    Final exam scores in a statistics class were normally distributed with a mean of \(63\) and a standard deviation of \(5\text{,}\) so \(X \sim N(63, 5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Find the probability that a randomly selected student scored more than \(65\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) Find the probability that a randomly selected student scored less than \(85\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (c) Find the 90th percentile—the score \(k\) with \(90\%\) of scores below it.
    🔗
    (d) Find the 70th percentile.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) Sketch the curve and shade to the right of \(65\text{.}\) Using normalcdf(65, 1E99, 63, 5) = 0.3446.
    🔗
    You can also standardize first: \(z = \dfrac{65 - 63}{5} = 0.4\text{.}\) The area to the left of \(z = 0.4\) is \(0.6554\text{,}\) so the area to the right is \(1 - 0.6554 = 0.3446\text{.}\)
    🔗
    About \(34.5\%\) of students scored above \(65\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (b) normalcdf(-1E99, 85, 63, 5) \approx 1. A score of \(85\) is more than four standard deviations above the mean, so essentially every student scored below it.
    🔗
    (c) Here you are given an area and want a value, so use invNorm(0.90, 63, 5) = 69.4.
    🔗
    The 90th percentile is \(69.4\text{:}\) ninety percent of scores fall at or below \(69.4\text{,}\) and ten percent at or above. A value like this is often called a critical value.
    🔗
    (d) invNorm(0.70, 63, 5) = 65.6. Seventy percent of scores fall at or below \(65.6\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3.5.

    Heights are normally distributed with \(\mu = 64\) inches and \(\sigma = 3\) inches. You want to find the height that separates the shortest \(25\%\) from everyone else. Which calculation does the job?
    🔗
    • invNorm(0.25, 64, 3)
    • Correct. You were handed an area and asked for a value, so you need the inverse.
    • normalcdf(-1E99, 0.25, 64, 3)
    • This treats \(0.25\) as a height rather than as an area. Check Table 2.3.3.
    • normalcdf(25, 1E99, 64, 3)
    • This finds the probability of a height above \(25\) inches, which is not what was asked.
    • invNorm(0.75, 64, 3)
    • Close, but invNorm always takes the area to the left. The shortest \(25\%\) is a left area of \(0.25\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Example 2.3.6. Computer use at home.

    Suppose the average time a household personal computer is used for entertainment is two hours per day, with times normally distributed and a standard deviation of half an hour, so \(X \sim N(2, 0.5)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    (a) Find the probability that such a computer is used for entertainment between \(1.8\) and \(2.75\) hours per day.
    🔗
    (b) Find the maximum number of hours per day used by the bottom quartile of households.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) You want the area between two values, so both endpoints go straight into the command: normalcdf(1.8, 2.75, 2, 0.5) = 0.5886.
    🔗
    (b) The bottom quartile is the lowest \(25\%\text{,}\) so find \(k\) with \(P(X \lt k) = 0.25\text{:}\) invNorm(0.25, 2, 0.5) = 1.66.
    🔗
    The bottom quartile of households uses the computer at most \(1.66\) hours per day for entertainment.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3.7.

    Golf scores for a school team are normally distributed with mean \(68\) and standard deviation \(3\text{.}\) What is the probability that a randomly selected golfer scored less than \(65\text{?}\)
    🔗
    • About \(0.1587\)
    • Correct. \(65\) is exactly one standard deviation below the mean, and about \(16\%\) of a normal distribution lies below that point. (normalcdf(-1E99, 65, 68, 3).)
    • About \(0.8413\)
    • That is the area to the right of \(65\text{.}\) The question asks for scores less than \(65\text{.}\)
    • About \(0.3174\)
    • That is the total area in both tails beyond one standard deviation. You want only the left one.
    • About \(0.68\)
    • The Empirical Rule’s \(68\%\) is the area between \(-1\) and \(1\) standard deviations, not a tail area.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.3.3 Percentiles and interpretation

    A percentile is only useful if you can say what it means in words. The sentence pattern is always the same: p percent of the population is at or below this value, and the remaining percent is at or above it.
    🔗

    Example 2.3.8. Ages of smartphone users.

    In the United States, the ages of smartphone users aged 13 to 55+ approximately follow a normal distribution with mean \(36.9\) years and standard deviation \(13.9\) years.
    🔗
    (a) Find the probability that a randomly selected user is between \(23\) and \(64.7\) years old.
    🔗
    (b) Find the probability that a user is at most \(50.8\) years old.
    🔗
    (c) Find the 80th percentile and interpret it in a complete sentence.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) normalcdf(23, 64.7, 36.9, 13.9) = 0.8186
    🔗
    (b) normalcdf(-1E99, 50.8, 36.9, 13.9) = 0.8413
    🔗
    (c) invNorm(0.80, 36.9, 13.9) = 48.6
    🔗
    The 80th percentile is \(48.6\) years. Eighty percent of smartphone users aged 13 to 55+ are \(48.6\) years old or younger, and twenty percent are \(48.6\) or older.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3.9.

    For the smartphone users in Example 2.3.8, the 30th percentile is \(29.6\) years. Write one complete sentence interpreting this number for someone who has never taken a statistics course.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Thirty percent of smartphone users aged 13 to 55+ are \(29.6\) years old or younger, and the other seventy percent are \(29.6\) years old or older.
    🔗
    A good interpretation names the percentage, the value, the population, and the direction. Saying only “the 30th percentile is \(29.6\)” restates the arithmetic without explaining it.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.3.10. Quartiles and the interquartile range.

    Using the same smartphone distribution, \(X \sim N(36.9, 13.9)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    (a) Calculate the interquartile range.
    🔗
    (b) Forty percent of users are at least what age?
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) The quartiles are the 25th and 75th percentiles:
    🔗
    invNorm(0.75, 36.9, 13.9) = 46.2754, so \(Q_3 = 46.2754\text{.}\)
    🔗
    invNorm(0.25, 36.9, 13.9) = 27.5246, so \(Q_1 = 27.5246\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} IQR = Q_3 - Q_1 = 46.2754 - 27.5246 = 18.7508 \end{equation*}
    (b) “At least” means “greater than or equal to,” so \(0.40\) is the area to the right. Since invNorm wants a left area, convert first: \(1 - 0.40 = 0.60\text{.}\)
    🔗
    invNorm(0.60, 36.9, 13.9) = 40.4215
    🔗
    Forty percent of users are at least \(40.42\) years old.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3.11.

    Exam scores are normal with \(\mu = 81\) and \(\sigma = 15\text{.}\) You want the score that the top \(20\%\) of students exceeded. What area should you give invNorm?
    🔗
    • \(0.80\)
    • Correct. The top \(20\%\) is a right area, and invNorm requires the area to the left: \(1 - 0.20 = 0.80\text{.}\)
    • \(0.20\)
    • That would give the score separating the bottom \(20\%\text{.}\) Convert the right area to a left area first.
    • \(0.50\)
    • That returns the mean, which splits the distribution in half.
    • \(20\)
    • invNorm takes a probability between \(0\) and \(1\text{,}\) not a percentage.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 2.3.4 Finding a middle interval

    A question like “the middle \(20\%\) of values fall between what two numbers?” requires one extra step. The middle area is centered on the mean, so whatever is left over splits evenly between the two tails.
    🔗

    Strategy for a middle interval.

    To find the middle \(p\) of a distribution, first compute the leftover area \(1 - p\text{,}\) then split it in half. Each tail gets \(\dfrac{1 - p}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The lower endpoint is at left area \(\dfrac{1 - p}{2}\text{.}\) The upper endpoint is at left area \(\dfrac{1 - p}{2} + p\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 2.3.12. Mandarin oranges.

    A citrus farmer finds that the diameters of mandarin oranges from his farm follow a normal distribution with mean \(5.85\) cm and standard deviation \(0.24\) cm.
    🔗
    (a) Find the probability that a randomly selected orange has diameter larger than \(6.0\) cm.
    🔗
    (b) The middle \(20\%\) of oranges have diameters between what two values?
    🔗
    (c) Find the 90th percentile and interpret it.
    🔗
    Solution.
    (a) normalcdf(6, 1E99, 5.85, 0.24) = 0.2660. About \(26.6\%\) of the oranges are larger than \(6.0\) cm.
    🔗
    (b) The leftover area is \(1 - 0.20 = 0.80\text{,}\) so each tail has area \(0.40\text{.}\) The lower endpoint is the 40th percentile and the upper endpoint is the 60th percentile.
    🔗
    invNorm(0.40, 5.85, 0.24) = 5.79 cm
    🔗
    invNorm(0.60, 5.85, 0.24) = 5.91 cm
    🔗
    The middle \(20\%\) of oranges are between \(5.79\) cm and \(5.91\) cm in diameter.
    🔗
    (c) invNorm(0.90, 5.85, 0.24) = 6.16 cm. Ninety percent of the mandarin oranges from this farm have a diameter of \(6.16\) cm or less.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Checkpoint 2.3.13.

    You want the middle \(50\%\) of a normal distribution. What are the two left areas you feed to invNorm?
    🔗
    • \(0.25\) and \(0.75\)
    • Correct. The leftover area is \(0.50\text{,}\) split into two tails of \(0.25\) each. These two values are exactly \(Q_1\) and \(Q_3\text{.}\)
    • \(0.50\) and \(1.00\)
    • That describes the entire upper half, not a middle interval. Review Strategy for a middle interval.
    • \(0.00\) and \(0.50\)
    • That is the lower half of the distribution, and \(0.00\) has no finite value attached to it.
    • \(0.50\) and \(0.50\)
    • Both of these return the mean, giving an interval of zero width.
    🔗
    🔗
    Probability for a normal distribution is area under the curve. \(P(X \lt x)\) is the area to the left, and \(P(X \gt x) = 1 - P(X \lt x)\) is the area to the right. For a continuous distribution, endpoints do not matter: \(P(X \lt x) = P(X \le x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Given a value and asked for a probability, use normalcdf. Given a probability and asked for a value, use invNorm, which always takes the area to the left. For a middle interval of area \(p\text{,}\) each tail holds \(\dfrac{1-p}{2}\text{.}\)
